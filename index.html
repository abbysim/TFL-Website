Anticipating Strategic Technological Surprise

The future rarely arrives as expected. The Tech Futures Lab helps leaders better anticipate potential strategic technological surprises and build institutions capable of adapting when the future takes a different path.

The many technological advances under way—from artificial intelligence and bioengineering to quantum technologies, energy innovations, and space capabilities—are accelerating the pace of change and introducing a new era of outsized possibilities and uncharted risks.

New technologies can produce strategic surprises in a variety of ways

⏱

Pace of Emergence

Capabilities arrive faster—or slower—than institutions are prepared for.

🎯

Unintended Applications

Tools built for one purpose get repurposed for another, without warning.

🌐

Unlikely Adopters & Winners

Advantage doesn't always accrue to the actor everyone expected.

🔄

Convergence of Technologies

Two unremarkable capabilities combine into something nobody modeled.

📈

Secondary & Tertiary Consequences

The first-order effect is rarely the one that ends up mattering most.

China's increased technological competencies and the rapid diffusion of capabilities across borders are compounding this uncertainty and increasing the chance of Sputnik-like strategic shocks that could leave the U.S. at a disadvantage.

Mitigating the Element of Surprise

Although eliminating surprise is impossible, foresight can help identify a range of possible disruptions and give decision makers the information and time needed to prepare to mitigate or take advantage of them. The Tech Futures Lab applies a range of foresight approaches to identify consequential technological developments, uncover blind spots, and anticipate potential surprises before they become strategic disruptions:

📚

Learn from Previous Strategic Surprises

Study past strategic surprises—from Sputnik to 9/11—to identify recurring patterns in how warnings are missed.

🌫

Recognize Uncertainty

Distinguish what is genuinely unknowable from what is merely under-examined, and resource each differently.

🔍

Identify Assumptions and Biases

Ask not just what we know, but what we have stopped questioning.

🧠

Explore and Imagine

Use structured and unstructured scenario exercises to examine the origin, trajectory, and consequences of technological surprises.

♟

Think Like the Adversary/Competitor

Put ourselves in the competitor's shoes and examine how others might develop, adopt, apply, or adapt technologies under real-world constraints.

📡

Scan Widely

Break down bureaucratic barriers and information silos by continuously monitoring academic publications, patents, international commercial activity, and other open sources for emerging capabilities and novel applications.

⚙

Apply Tech Against Tech

Use AI to improve the speed, breadth, and depth of foresight, scenario generation, wargaming, and analysis.

⚖

Reframe Accountability

Examine not only why warnings were missed, but why contingency plans were not developed and what costs followed from insufficient preparation.

Improving Resilience and Bureaucratic Flexibility

Forecasting surprise is only half the challenge. The Tech Futures Lab works with institutions to translate predictions into preparedness by developing the flexibility, capabilities, and response mechanisms needed to adapt when surprises unfold.

🤝

Build Trusted, Expert Teams

Assemble diverse, expert teams capable of moving quickly to determine the risks and advantages of an evolving situation.

🎭

Institutionalize a Policy Devil's Advocate

Create a contrarian voice capable of challenging conventional thinking and compelling preparation for seemingly low-probability events and trends.

📋

Develop Response Plans

Create and rehearse domain-specific response plans that can be deployed quickly when a surprise occurs.

📜

Establish Protocols and Norms Before Technologies Emerge

Develop governance frameworks, regulatory approaches, and response protocols before emerging technologies force decisions under pressure.

📣

Prepare Communication Plans and Procedures

Establish trusted messengers, communication channels, and clear procedures for sharing information when situations evolve rapidly.

From Foresight to Action

The Tech Futures Lab puts these ideas into practice through state-of-the-art foresight exercises with researchers, innovators, investors, and government leaders—drawing out new insights, identifying vulnerabilities and opportunities, and developing strategies to mitigate risk while maximizing advantages.

Through scenario development, wargaming, and speculative, alternative, and sometimes contrarian research, the Lab creates opportunities to examine how emerging technologies could transform our world in unplanned and unexpected ways.

Beyond the Obvious

Unconventional Thinking about Emerging Technologies

1 September 2026

The Hoover Institution launched the Tech Futures Lab to help anticipate and prepare for potential strategic technological surprises in an era of accelerating change. To fulfill this mandate, each month the Tech Futures Lab will explore the horizon of emerging technologies to highlight some interesting developments, interrogate underlying assumptions, identify weak signals, and ask critical questions about plausible–not necessarily probable–implications. The TFL welcomes your feedback on topics to pursue in greater depth.

Highlights

In Below the Headlines, the TFL highlights a range of technological advancements which may not have hit mainstream media, including how the convergence of existing and advancing technologies is improving demining operations in Ukraine and how AI-driven autonomous labs are enabling rapid scientific discovery and prototyping.

In Fragile Assumptions, we question the premise that the best AI model will win big and sustain that success over time.

Then we surface a number of Uncomfortable Questions—What Ifs—about the uses and implications of emerging technologies. This month we ask how AI might affect national power and competitive advantages over time. These questions may become deeper dives in future TFL analysis.

Below the Headlines

Here are four developments in emerging tech which may not have hit your inbox but are quietly transforming medicine, defense, environmental restoration, and research. Most of these are not a single new technology, but the convergence of existing and advancing technologies to enable new and better capabilities.

Materials

Engineering Electronic Skin to Jumpstart Healing

Researchers are developing ultrathin, bio-electronics capable of sending targeted electrical currents directly into damaged tissue to jumpstart cellular growth. The result is a stretchable “e-skin” that speeds up recovery times for severe, hard-to-heal injuries.

SEMI PMC HZDR
Security

Clearing Conflict Zones with Drone Networks

Faced with millions of acres of landmines, engineers are pairing autonomous drones with AI threat-mapping, precision GPS, and robots to spot hidden explosives and clear them quickly and safely. This high-speed crisis response is setting a brand-new standard for post-conflict recovery.

United Nations Ukraine
Environment

Terraforming Earth to Rehabilitate Ecosystems

Scientists are hardening photosynthetic bacteria through extreme heat and drought stress, then using synthetic biology to engineer drought-resistant “biocrusts.” Supported by ecological world models to prevent unintended native ecosystem collapse, these custom microbes are designed to trap moisture, stabilize soil, and halt desertification.

CSIC Noema
Research

Automating Science with AI-Driven Autonomous Labs

AI platforms are moving beyond data analysis to execute complete experimental loops—formulating hypotheses, running robotic workbenches, and analyzing results without human intervention. The mundane aspects of laboratory work could be fully automated, leaving humans to focus on high-level conceptual breakthroughs. By deploying AI agents and “digital twin” simulations across interconnected labs, researchers could create a 24/7 automated engine for rapid scientific discovery, and by integrating sensors with automated synthesis, labs could theoretically detect a new biomarker and immediately begin manufacturing drugs to address it without human intervention.

Nature Argonne Carnegie Mellon

Canaries in the Coal Mine

Early signs of transformation are rarely the emergence of a new technology or capability but instead are small, persistent changes in human behavior. Individually, these changes may be of little consequence, but together they can reveal a real systemic change, starting in one domain and possibly cascading into many others.

Anticipating the Future of Human Labor in the Age of Automation

Automation has long been framed as a race to remove humans from the loop. A growing collection of technical releases and software protocols points toward a more complicated future.

As AI systems move beyond software, they are beginning to recruit, coordinate, and embed human labor into their own operations. Humans may remain in the loop while becoming less visible.

⌂
MCP marketplaces

Meatspace Bounties

Platforms such as RentAHuman connect Model Context Protocol endpoints to human gig workers, letting autonomous agents find, contract with, and pay people through APIs and crypto rails.

Agents can outsource physical tasks such as photographing storefronts, verifying inventory, inspecting locations, or confirming offline events.

🛡
1X NEO

In-Home Teleoperation

NEO’s “Scheduled Expert Mode” allows a remote human operator wearing a VR headset to take control when onboard autonomy encounters a task it cannot reliably complete.

Autonomy can fall back to human judgment and dexterity while the customer still experiences a single robotic interface.

🔎
OpenClaw

Unsolicited Human Sensing Networks

Agents with device-level access to calendars, messaging, and web automation can initiate contact with people without a human operator directing each interaction.

Documented agents called local businesses to survey inventory and ingredients, turning members of the public into an ad hoc physical sensing network.

⚒
Caterpillar Cat Command

Industrial Tele-Command Hubs

Remote operators can pilot excavators, dozers, and wheel loaders through real-time video and low-latency telemetry from off-site command stations.

Systems developed for mining and hazardous sites have expanded into construction, separating physical work from the worker’s location.

Trajectory
→

The path to autonomy may not be a straight line from human-operated to fully autonomous systems. A more likely model is hybrid: people handle the judgment, dexterity, and exception handling that keep machines running.

→

Unintended Consequences

Homogenization of thought

If millions of people depend on the same handful of models for writing, brainstorming, and decision support, the variance of human output could shrink, even as the average quality rises. This could result in fewer novel ideas, fewer genuine outliers, and more convergence toward whatever the model considers to be reasonable.

Indicators to monitor: Increasingly uniform email and text responses, AI generated new accounts, layoffs of journalists

Irrevocable biometric compromise

As fingerprints, iris scans, and voice/face ID become the backbone of authentication, a data breach could permanently compromise a person’s identity, wealth, and health. You can't reset your face or fingerprint like you can reset a password. Systems built for convenience are quietly creating unrecoverable failure modes.

Space—the Next Legal Frontier

With more competing systems, actors, and missions in space and relatively few laws, norms, and international institutions, the number of disputes between governments and companies is likely to increase rapidly. The OST of 1967 only specifies that private space corporations must be regulated by the countries they are based in. There are no guidelines on how to navigate tensions between the government and private sectors. These disputes are likely to lead to greater demand for space law expertise in the near future.

Uncomfortable Questions

If AI is ubiquitous, how are knowledge, expertise, and capabilities assessed?

If experimentation becomes abundant, does scientific prestige shift from discovering ideas toward validating them? Can AI be first author?

If automated laboratories become globally accessible, does geography become less important for innovation, or more important because physical infrastructure concentrates?

If AI models increasingly build themselves, then could every country have sovereign AI over time or perhaps, no country will have a sovereign AI?

More broadly, what are the future sources of competitive advantage as AI spreads globally?

What if technological innovations outpace human ability to absorb and adopt it?

What if attention, trust, and meaning turn out to be the actual scarce resources of the next few decades?

Can deterrence be established with AGI?

Report · Methodology

Anticipating and Navigating Strategic Technological Surprise

A framework for how the Tech Futures Lab identifies, tracks, and prepares for strategic surprises as new technologies emerge and converge.

By Maria Langan Riekhof and Herb Lin · May 26, 2026
↧  Download PDF

The many technological advances underway in artificial intelligence, bioengineering, quantum technologies, energy innovations, and space capabilities are accelerating the pace and scale of change and introducing a new era of outsized possibilities and uncharted risks.

New discoveries and technologies have always been disruptive, and some have been broadly transformative, from the wheel and the printing press to the automobile and the internet. However, today's convergence of advancing technologies and strong economic incentives is leading to new technological applications and disruptions at breathtaking speed. Many important aspects of human life are being altered, forcing individuals, societies, institutions, and governments to adapt.

These technological disruptions are strategically significant when they overturn prevailing assumptions, dramatically alter the course of events, empower actors and shift advantage, and require significant responses at the societal, national, and/or international level. For example, China's unveiling of DeepSeek last year was a strategic shock to the U.S. policy community because it overturned the assumption that China could not produce advanced, globally competitive AI at scale.

With the rapid pace of technological development and diffusion, it is increasingly risky for governments, industries, and communities not to anticipate and prepare for how new discoveries and capabilities will be used and how they will impact their interests, security, and daily lives. Once new technologies are unleashed, it may be too late to shape their applications or adequately prepare for likely implications. Strategic warning of their potential emergence, unintended applications, and unanticipated implications is increasingly essential.

To help leaders navigate this era of rapid innovation, Hoover's Tech Futures Lab offers a provisional framework for thinking about future strategic technological surprises. Our framework offers criteria for defining strategic surprise, identifies different categories of technological surprise across the innovation lifecycle, widens the aperture beyond traditional military contexts, and suggests multiple approaches to better anticipate and prepare for disruptions. Rather than being definitive, we provide this framework as a starting point for conversations about the risks and opportunities of technological disruptions.

Protecting and maintaining U.S. security and prosperity in the years ahead requires consistent efforts both to reduce the element of ‘surprise’ and to make our societies, economy, and country more resilient and adaptive.

Report · Methodology

Inaugural Retreat Highlights: Exploring Unprecedented Futures

Overarching themes and cross-cutting debates from the Tech Futures Lab's first retreat.

By Maria Langan Riekhof and Emerson Johnston · June 2026
↧  Download PDF

On June 23–24, 2026, the Hoover Institution's Tech Futures Lab hosted its inaugural retreat, which brought together thought leaders from academia, industry, nonprofits, and governments to help anticipate and mitigate the risk of strategic technological surprises. Through a series of interconnected exercises, this brain trust identified possible technological breakthroughs, debated controversial assumptions about AI and national power, traced potential unplanned and unexpected applications of technology, and considered the long-term effects of technology on multiple domains, including health, economics, geopolitics, and humanity.

The retreat was not designed to make specific forecasts but to explore a broader range of plausible—not necessarily probable—trajectories for existing and emerging technologies. The insights will help set the agenda for the Lab in the coming year, including topics for future wargames and exercises, and provocative questions to address in future analytic products.

The following summary presents the overarching themes and cross-cutting debates that surfaced organically during almost every discussion and exercise over the course of the two days.

The Past May No Longer Be Prologue

Throughout the retreat, participants raised the possibility that we could be facing an unprecedented future in which events and dynamics have no historical analog. As a result, longstanding models and theoretical frameworks for analyzing and understanding strategic domains, including the economy, governance, and geopolitics, may no longer be relevant as the players change, sources of power expand and evolve, and humans are taken out of the equation. For instance, how do standard models of coercion and crisis management apply if the decision-maker is AI or is heavily reliant on AI in ways we might not know?

Technological Advantages—Invention, Adoption, or Scale

A key discussion running through the retreat was on technology and strategic advantage. Participants discussed what provides the most sustainable advantage—invention, adoption, or scale of production—and for whom. From the debates, many participants seemed persuaded that diffusion and deployment may matter more to national strength than invention. A country that invents a technology may not be the country that manufactures, scales, or sells it most effectively. Similarly, within societies, adoption and adaptation may pay as many dividends as invention and without the high investment cost of research and development. Additional questions included: How might advantages in development vs. deployment be different in different technological areas? Where does it pay most to be first? Where do benefits accrue most from fast following—and why? Can advantage be sustained over time?

AI as Foundation, Infrastructure, and Multiplier

Artificial intelligence appeared in almost every discussion, even when the assigned subject was robotics, biotechnology, government, education, finance, or defense. Most participants believed that AI is likely to evolve from a distinct product into an operating layer that simply becomes part of underlying infrastructure. Much like electricity and the internet, AI was assumed to become part of the infrastructure upon which everything else is being built. However, many raised concerns about access and cost creating disparities within and between countries.

The Future of Power

Most participants agreed that technology has become a more important source of power, influence, and authority in the international system, but not just for states. Historically, communications networks, space capabilities, energy infrastructure, currencies, surveillance tools, weapons, and computational capabilities were built or controlled mainly by governments, but today and going forward, private companies and individuals lead, own, and operate these advanced systems at a scale that gives them direct influence over public decisions and international regimes, standards, and institutions. This network power gives nonstate actors influence that crosses national boundaries and in some cases, supersedes the power of the state.

U.S. Power and Influence. Participants saw several ways the United States could lose its leading position in science and technology, including cuts to universities and basic science research, hostility toward foreign talent, weak manufacturing capacity, poor communication about technology, badly designed regulation, declining domestic public trust in technology that impedes adoption, eroding trust among allies, and excessive reliance on proprietary frontier models. The combination of these dynamics is likely to weaken both U.S. hard and soft power.

Role of Nonstate Actors. Participants considered how technologies are increasing the power and reach of nonstate actors in different domains, including a company controlling a satellite network affecting military communications; a model provider setting rules for access to information or analytical capability; and a firm with private energy, security, and digital infrastructure becoming less dependent on the state. In many ways, these nonstate actors have the potential to possess sovereignty without physical territory. Participants argued that such dynamics could limit the power of non-authoritarian states.

The Future of Governance

Participants were divided over the impact of technology on the effectiveness and legitimacy of governments and governing systems. On the one hand, automated systems might make public services faster and easier to use, leading to more efficient processes and benefits. Agencies could detect some forms of fraud, process information more quickly, and tailor services to individuals. On the other hand, the same systems could exclude eligible people, make errors that are difficult to appeal, or move public decisions behind trade-secret protections. Similarly, there was debate over surveillance and automation and whether they would make governments more effective but less legitimate. Cameras and data analysis could reduce crime and fraud and make people safer—as in authoritarian, security states—but at significant cost to freedom and liberty.

What Is True

Several participants anticipated that policy formulation could become increasingly unstable when agreement is eroding on even the most basic facts of many issues. The conversation went beyond concerns about synthetic media and deepfakes, describing the defining challenge as not only detecting fakery but establishing mechanisms of verification and accountability durable enough to function when truth itself is contested and/or emergent. Moreover, there is a risk that public debate among science and technology experts can blur the lines between evidence, opinion, and emotion, fueling public pessimism and distrust in science itself.

The Role of Trust

Connected to truth, trust emerged as one of the retreat's main concerns. Specifically, how might technologies contribute to the ongoing erosion of trust in leaders, institutions, expertise and information more generally? Participants discussed synthetic media, cyberattacks, automated persuasion, biased models, opaque government systems, and information environments in which people cannot tell whether a source is genuine. The problem is broader than false content. A flood of generated material can weaken confidence in accurate information, and even authentic recordings can be dismissed as fake. Governments can use the presence of disinformation to justify more extensive controls. Private platforms can become the de facto arbiters of which claims are seen and which are suppressed, but private companies have competing agendas that do not necessarily reflect the public interest.

What May Be Lost

Many of the conversations raised the question of what we may be losing as we adopt more advanced technologies, especially AI. Participants raised concerns about the erosion of human capabilities, especially learning skills and critical thinking, both of which require a degree of friction rather than instant answers. Rather than augmenting and enhancing human capability, could we lose capacity and skills over time as we cede thinking processes to AI? Connected to this line of questioning were other debates about ethical decision-making, especially related to human life in warfare and in medical treatments, and about human purpose if AI and automation eliminate jobs. What remains distinctly human once machines can do the rest? Many participants were eager to continue these discussions.

New Vocabulary
